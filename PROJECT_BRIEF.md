## Goal

Synthetic respondents generated by large language models (LLMs) are now being used in place of real
survey respondents. The package tests how authentic they are: Whether they resemble the data people
can actually collect. It does this with three anchored indices and benchmarks several LLMs on them.

- **Overlap:** Do synthetic respondents land where real people land? (Ported from mirtverse.)
- **Structure:** Do the items relate to each other as they do in real people? (Ported from mirtverse.)
- **Behaviour (new):** Do synthetic respondents answer the way real people do, including variability,
  endpoint and midpoint use, agreeing, inconsistency and person misfit?

The output is the **synthetic authenticity benchmark**: One table with a row per model and the three
anchored columns, plus a single benchmark score.

The first study benchmarks six models on the HEXACO data (IPIP-HEXACO, 240 items, 7-point scale,
Canada, Great Britain and the United States): Gemma 4 26B, GPT-OSS-120B, Qwen 3.8 27B,
Qwen 3.5 122B, plus Claude and ChatGPT (new data from Okan).

## Decisions already made (Okan, 2026-11-12)

1. **Target:** All real respondents by default, because authenticity means resembling the data people
   can actually collect. `clean_real = TRUE` compares against real respondents after careless cases
   are removed.
2. **Scoring:** Three anchored columns, with the weakest column as the single benchmark score by
   default. Users can change the score (see `score` below).
3. **Behaviour features:** Exactly the eight features below. Response time, open-ended text features
   and similar are out of scope for the package. They could be added later by users.
4. **Names:** The table is the "synthetic authenticity benchmark". The new column is "Behaviour".
5. **Data:** Combine the four existing models with the new Claude and ChatGPT data into one dataset,
   six models in total.
6. **Later, not now:** Functions that blend models to make a more authentic mix (mirtverse already has
   `blend_llms()`, which can be ported in phase 3).
7. A separate GitHub repository that Okan creates. The package stands on its own and does not
   depend on mirtverse.

## Inputs (same shape as mirtverse)

- `synthetic`: A named list with one element per model. Each element is a list of attempts, and each
  attempt is a data frame of responses (one row per synthetic respondent, items as columns).
- `real`: A data frame of real responses with the same item columns.
- `items`: An optional item table with `item`, `scale` (the trait), `facet` (optional) and `reverse`
  (logical). The HEXACO data ships one as `hexaco_items` (columns `item`, `trait`, `facet`,
  `reverse`, ...).
- `group`: An optional grouping column (for example `country`), with `within_group` as in mirtverse.
- Missing values: `NA`. In the raw HEXACO real data, 0 means missing.

## The eight behaviour features (per respondent)

All features are computed on raw answers unless the text says "keyed". Keyed means reverse-keyed
items are recoded as `(min + max) - x` (for HEXACO: `8 - x`).

| Feature | Definition | Careless direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Variability | SD of the respondent's raw answers across all items | Very low (straight-lining) or very high (random) |
| Extreme | Share of answers at the lowest or highest category | High |
| Midpoint | Share of answers at the middle category (odd-length scales only) | High |
| Acquiescence | Mean raw answer minus the scale midpoint, over all items | High (yes-saying) |
| Key inconsistency | Mean over facets of the absolute difference between the keyed mean of positive items and the keyed mean of reverse items in that facet | High |
| Even-odd consistency | Correlation across facets between the keyed means of odd-numbered and even-numbered items within each facet | Low |
| Mahalanobis distance | Square root of the Mahalanobis distance of the keyed facet means, using the real data's mean and covariance | High |
| Person fit lz | Standardized log-likelihood (Drasgow) under a graded response model fitted to the real data, one model per scale, averaged over scales | Low (misfit); very high means "too coherent" |

Notes:

- Without facets, use scales in place of facets for key inconsistency, even-odd consistency and
  Mahalanobis distance.
- Person fit: Fit `mirt(real_keyed[, scale_items], 1, "graded")` once per scale. For every
  respondent, real or synthetic, take the MAP theta on a grid (-5 to 5, 81 points, standard normal
  prior) using the real item parameters. Then compute
  `lz = (l0 - E) / sqrt(V)`, where `l0` is the log-likelihood of the observed answers and `E` and
  `V` are its expectation and variance at that theta. The reference implementation is in
  `reference/behaviour_pilot.R`. Do not refit the model on synthetic data. Synthetic data often
  skip categories, which breaks mirt's parameter tables.
- Skip a feature for a respondent when fewer than half of the needed answers are present.
- Longstring is left out (not one of the eight). Its integer values with many ties capped the real
  split-half overlap at 46 in the pilot.

## Behaviour overlap

For each feature, cut the real values at their deciles and count the share of synthetic respondents
in each decile. This is exactly how mirtverse computes Overlap for score deciles. Then:

```
B_f       = 100 * sum over deciles d of min(p_syn[f, d], 0.10)
Behaviour = mean of B_f over the eight features
```

Ties: Before assigning deciles, break ties at the cut points randomly (seeded). This matters for
Extreme and Midpoint on short scales, where many respondents share the same value.

Report each `B_f` too, with a direction: whether the synthetic median is below or above the real
median. That direction is what tells a reader "too tidy" or "too noisy".

**Two-tailed person fit line.** Report the share of synthetic respondents below the real data's
5th percentile of lz (careless-like) and above its 95th percentile (too coherent). Real data are
5% and 5% by construction. The pilot gave 0.0% to 0.1% careless-like for every model.

## Anchoring (floor and ceiling)

Every column (Overlap, Structure, Behaviour) is rescaled:

```
A = 100 * (X_model - X_floor) / (X_ceiling - X_floor)
```

- **Floor:** A dumb generator. Draw each item column independently from the real data (with
  replacement), with the same number of respondents as the synthetic attempt. It keeps every item's
  distribution but breaks people apart. Average over 20 draws.
- **Ceiling:** Split the real data at random into two halves and score one half against the other
  with the same index. Average over 20 splits (`n_splits`). When the real sample is large, use halves
  of the same size as a typical synthetic attempt, so that sampling noise is comparable. This is the
  default `ceiling_n = "attempt"`, with `"half"` as the alternative.
- Report the raw value and the anchored value. Plots clamp anchored values to 0 to 100, but tables
  show them unclamped (below 0 means worse than shuffled columns).

## The benchmark score

`score = "weakest"` (default) takes the lowest of the three anchored columns and names which column
it was. The alternatives are `"mean"`, `"geometric"` (geometric mean of the clamped values), or a
user function of the three anchored values. `weights` (a named vector) works with `"mean"` and
`"geometric"`.

## clean_real = TRUE

Flag a real respondent as careless when at least two of the eight features fall in the real data's
most extreme 2.5% on the careless side (see the table above). `careless_rule` lets users supply their
own function, and the flags are returned so that users can inspect them. Then use the remaining real
respondents as the target for all three columns and for the anchors.

## Functions to build

| Function | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| `behaviour_features(data, items, real = NULL, fit_scales = NULL)` | The eight features per respondent. The real data's means, covariance and GRM parameters are used as the reference. |
| `synthetic_behaviour(synthetic, real, items, fit_scales = NULL, clean_real = FALSE, seed = 1)` | `B_f` and Behaviour per model and attempt, with directions and the person fit tails. |
| `synthetic_coverage()` and `gg_coverage()` | Ported from mirtverse (Overlap and Structure, score, theta, group and item slices). |
| `authenticity_benchmark(synthetic, real, items, score = "weakest", weights = NULL, clean_real = FALSE, n_splits = 20, ceiling_n = "attempt", seed = 1)` | The synthetic authenticity benchmark table. One row per model, with raw and anchored Overlap, Structure and Behaviour, the score, the weakest column, and the lowest and highest values across attempts. |
| `gg_behaviour(x)` | Behaviour strip. One row per model (or attempt), one column per feature, coloured by direction (red = synthetic lower than real, blue = higher) with strength = 100 - B_f. |
| `gg_benchmark(x)` | The benchmark table as a plot. Models sorted by score, the three anchored columns as coloured tiles, and the weakest column marked. |
| `flag_careless(real, items, rule = NULL)` | The careless flags used by `clean_real`. |
| `print()`, `tidy()`, `autoplot()` | For every result object. |

Keep the plots simple. Okan prefers one readable picture over layered, dense plots. Use the coverage
strip palette `c("#c0302b", "#f0a09a", "#f0efec", "#86b6ef", "#1c5cab")` and a theme equivalent to
mirtverse's `theme_mirtverse()` (copy it into the package).

## Datasets

- `hexaco_real`: 17,874 real respondents (Canada 1,708, Great Britain 1,149, United States 15,017),
  241 columns (`country` + 240 items). From the Open-Source Psychometrics Project. The `.rda` file is
  in `data/`.
- `hexaco_llm`: The four existing models, 59,611 respondents, 243 columns (`model`, `country`,
  `attempt` + 240 items), 10 attempts per country per model. **To do:** Add the Claude and ChatGPT
  data from Okan in the same layout, so that the dataset holds six models.
- `hexaco_items`: Item, trait, trait_label, facet, facet_label, statement, reverse.
- Answers are raw 1 to 7, so reverse-keyed items must be keyed with `8 - x`.

## Validation targets from the pilot

The pilot (`reference/behaviour_pilot.R`) used Canada, all 10 attempts, all 240 items for the
features, and person fit on the Honesty-Humility items only. The package will average lz over all
six traits, so its numbers will differ a little. With `fit_scales = "H"` (person fit on Honesty-Humility only), it should
reproduce the pilot within about 2 points:

| Source | Behaviour (raw) | Median lz | Even-odd (median) | Extreme (median) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Real, split half | 94 | 0.46 | 0.71 | 18% |
| Qwen 3.5 122B | 48 | 1.08 | 0.91 | 12% |
| Qwen 3.8 27B | 42 | 1.48 | 0.90 | 3% |
| GPT-OSS-120B | 38 | 1.63 | 0.91 | 2% |
| Gemma 4 26B | 20 | 1.56 | 0.89 | 3% |

A logistic classifier on the eight features separated every model from real people (AUC 1.00, real
halves 0.50). That is why AUC is a check and not the index.

## Literature

The literature scan and the reasoning behind the index are in the Claude Doc
<https://claude.ai/artifact/EigjGTFZqzKponaVLJGv1k>. The closest prior work is the Psychometric
Similarity Score of Lukauskas & Šarkauskaitė (2026, arXiv:2608.14606). It covers aggregate response
styles but not person-level response behaviour, and it gives no two-tailed reading of person fit.
